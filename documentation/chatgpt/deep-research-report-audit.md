# Blunt Audit of the Canadian Metaresearch Data Challenge Proposal

## Bottom line

No. This proposal is **not** “100% scientifically and methodologically sound and perfect.” It is a strong draft, and it is better than the average hand-wavy bibliometric proposal, but it is **not yet airtight**. The two biggest reasons are simple: first, the boundaries of metaresearch are inherently dynamic and partly contestable, which makes any claim of perfect field capture false on its face; second, any OpenAlex-based design inherits real limits in metadata completeness, affiliation accuracy, language assignment, and document-type quality. ## What the draft gets right

The proposal’s strongest move is conceptual. It defines metaresearch as a broad “research-on-research” field and explicitly includes methods, reporting, reproducibility, evaluation, incentives, bibliometrics, scientometrics, scholarly communication, research integrity, and adjacent traditions. That is not a rhetorical flourish; it is consistent with a standard account of metaresearch as an umbrella field that interfaces with sociology of science, communication studies, bibliometrics, ethics, research integrity, and other domains. That breadth is a strength **if** it is operationalized properly. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn10view0

The second major strength is the choice to use OpenAlex as the backbone rather than as unquestioned ground truth. That is the correct posture. OpenAlex is an open catalog with an API and downloadable data, and recent assessment work suggests it can be a reliable alternative for some bibliometric uses, especially at broader levels such as country analysis. At the same time, the literature does **not** support treating it as complete or pristine, especially for institution-level work or for analyses sensitive to missing affiliations and metadata noise. Your draft more or less understands this, which puts it ahead of weaker proposals that mistake an open index for a perfect census. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn3view0turn4view0turn18academia3turn6academia2

The third strength is the emphasis on openness, reproducibility, and governance. The draft’s instinct to connect the deliverable to FAIR and, where relevant, to CARE and OCAP is methodologically mature. In the Canadian context this matters, because the Tri-Agency Research Data Management Policy explicitly ties research excellence to replicability, accessibility, FAIR-aligned stewardship, and distinctions-based management of First Nations, Métis, and Inuit data; it also explicitly notes that OCAP is one model for First Nations data governance, while recognizing that governance must be community-developed and not imposed generically. That is exactly the right direction. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn15view0turn12view0turn12view1turn13view1

## Why the draft is not yet scientifically airtight

The main problem is that the proposal is still **too broad at the boundary-setting stage**. Saying that a record is in scope if it studies research practices, scholarly communication, evaluation, openness, infrastructures, or related systems sounds inclusive, but it is not yet a clean operational definition. It risks mixing direct metaresearch with adjacent commentary, infrastructure description, professional reflection, and policy discourse. Ioannidis and colleagues explicitly note that the boundaries of metaresearch are dynamic and somewhat arbitrary and require continuous updates and refinements. That does **not** mean “anything vaguely about science goes in.” It means you need a tiered boundary, not a sentimental one. Right now, the draft still reads as if it wants to include everything and let validation save it later. That is weak design. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn10view0

The validation plan is also **underspecified**. The draft says it will estimate precision with manual annotation, estimate recall with curated seeds, and analyze false positives and false negatives. Fine. That is the right general shape. But it does not say how many records will be dual-coded, how disagreements will be resolved, what strata will be used, what success thresholds count as acceptable, or how uncertain records will be treated. In other words, it names validation without fully designing validation. A reviewer who cares about method will see that immediately. fileciteturn0file0

The Canadian linkage logic is another weak point. The draft says a record can qualify through at least one Canadian-affiliated author, a substantial empirical focus on Canada, or a clear connection to Canadian institutions, infrastructures, or funding contexts. That is directionally sensible, but as written it is far too permissive for clean dataset construction. One Canadian coauthor on a globally generic paper is not the same thing as a paper about the Canadian metaresearch landscape, and incomplete affiliation metadata in OpenAlex make this even messier. Recent evidence indicates that OpenAlex can perform reasonably well for comparative institutional analysis in relative terms, but affiliation metadata remain incomplete enough that absolute assignment errors matter. If you do not convert the Canadian criterion into a **tiered confidence model**, you will contaminate the corpus. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn6academia2turn18academia3

The inclusiveness logic is also not as strong as the draft thinks it is. A bilingual vocabulary is necessary, but it is not sufficient. OpenAlex has more balanced linguistic coverage than Web of Science, but language metadata are still imperfect and can overestimate English while underestimating other languages. Worse, keyword-based retrieval can still miss francophone scholarship that is topically relevant but lexically non-obvious. Work on cross-lingual scholarly retrieval using bilingual metadata from Érudit shows exactly why explicit multilingual retrieval design matters when English queries must recover French scholarly content. So the proposal is right to mention French, but it is still too close to a conventional English-first query strategy with French added on top. That is not robust enough for a challenge that explicitly values inclusiveness. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn6academia1turn23academia6turn17academia4

There is also a concrete technical problem: at least one part of the draft is already **stale**. The text refers to an OpenAlex “`/text/keywords`” style workflow, but current OpenAlex developer documentation says the `/text` endpoint has been removed and points users instead to current entity endpoints such as `/keywords`, plus supported operations like search, filtering, grouping, and “Tag Aboutness.” If you leave outdated API language in a proposal that is supposed to signal technical competence, you hand the panel an avoidable reason to doubt implementation readiness. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn4view0turn3view0

A further weakness is the lack of explicit control for **non-research and paratext contamination**. OpenAlex document typing is not flawless, and recent work has shown that a non-trivial share of records classified as articles or reviews can plausibly be reclassified as non-research contributions such as editorials or other paratext. For a metaresearch landscape study, this matters a lot, because the field contains many commentaries, editorials, forum pieces, and manifestos that are intellectually relevant but not analytically equivalent to empirical research articles. If you do not state, in advance, how you will separate empirical metaresearch, conceptual contributions, editorials, and infrastructure announcements, your downstream maps will be muddy. citeturn18academia0turn18academia2

## The non-negotiable methodological repairs

First, you need a **tiered inclusion model**. Stop pretending the field boundary is binary. Make it explicit. For example, one tier can cover direct metaresearch studies whose primary object is research practice, research systems, reporting, reproducibility, evaluation, incentives, or scholarly communication. A second tier can cover adjacent but clearly relevant contributions, such as empirically grounded STS or infrastructure studies. A third tier can cover contextual records, such as policy or infrastructure documents, that are useful for mapping ecosystems but should not be pooled with the core analytical corpus. Every released record should carry both an inclusion rationale and a confidence label. That is far more defensible than a single yes-or-no rule in a field whose boundaries are known to be partly fluid. citeturn10view0turn18academia0

Second, the retrieval design needs to become a **real pipeline**, not just a broad search idea. The strongest version will use multiple entry routes: a bilingual seed set of known works and authors, structured OpenAlex filtering, keyword and semantic search, citation expansion from the seed set, and explicit cross-lingual query refinement for French-facing sources. You should also freeze the extraction to an explicit OpenAlex source state and record API parameters, cursor logic, software environment, and transformation steps. OpenAlex’s own documentation makes clear that cursor-based deep pagination is required beyond the basic result cap and that reproducible bulk extraction depends on explicit handling of API or snapshot state. If this is not frozen and logged, it is not reproducible enough. citeturn10view0turn4view0turn3view0turn23academia6

Third, the validation plan needs to stop being generic and become **auditable**. At minimum, the final proposal should commit to a stratified validation design that slices errors by language, document type, research tradition, and Canadian linkage type. It should use dual independent coding for at least a sampled subset of records, document adjudication rules, and report error patterns rather than only headline precision and recall. It should also include a held-out seed set for recall stress-testing, because validating only on the records your search strategy already surfaces is methodologically lazy. Your draft gestures in this direction, but it does not yet lock the reviewer into a clear, checkable protocol. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn10view0

Fourth, if topic modeling or thematic clustering appears in the final submission, it must be clearly described as **secondary exploratory analysis**, not as the mechanism that defines the field. Topic modeling methods are known to vary across runs and preprocessing choices, and recent work continues to warn against treating learned topics as ground truth without reliability checks. If you include topic modeling, you should say explicitly that it will be run with stability assessment and used for descriptive mapping after the corpus has already been constructed through the validated retrieval-and-screening pipeline. Otherwise you risk looking flashy instead of serious. citeturn19academia0turn19academia1turn19academia3

Fifth, tighten the ethics and governance language. FAIR alone is not enough, and CARE/OCAP mention should not be decorative. In the Canadian policy context, data stewardship must respect Indigenous self-determination, free, prior, and informed consent where relevant, and community-developed governance for Indigenous data. The final proposal should therefore say something precise: that any labels, linkages, or derived subsets concerning Indigenous communities or governance-related content will be reviewed under appropriate principles and not released beyond what is ethically justified. That is stronger than dropping acronyms and hoping the panel is impressed. citeturn15view0turn12view1turn13view1turn13view3

## How the submission should be rewritten if you want to win

Right now, too much of the draft reads like a smart background memo and not enough of it reads like a **reviewer-optimized application**. The call is not rewarding literary fluency. It is rewarding methodological rigor, feasibility, reproducibility, inclusiveness, originality, and clarity. So the final version should be shorter, sharper, more operational, and less essayistic. It should tell the committee exactly what the unit of analysis is, exactly how candidate records are generated, exactly how inclusion decisions are made, exactly how accuracy is validated, and exactly what will be released. fileciteturn0file0

You should also stop hiding the key innovation deep inside the prose. The real differentiator here is **not** “we will use OpenAlex and do maps.” That is ordinary. The differentiator is: **we will build a versioned, confidence-scored, bilingual, bias-audited Canadian metaresearch corpus with explicit validation by language, tradition, document type, and Canadian linkage type**. That is the sentence the committee needs to remember. It is method-first, not dashboard-first. It also fits much better with the metaresearch literature’s emphasis on the need for broad but disciplined field-mapping rather than vague enthusiasm. citeturn10view0turn18academia3turn6academia1

One more blunt point: do **not** lean too hard on “inclusive” unless you can show the mechanism. Reviewers are tired of performative inclusiveness. If the proposal does not explicitly say how French-language retrieval will be audited, how underrepresented traditions will be checked for undercapture, how document-type contamination will be handled, and how Indigenous governance issues will be managed when relevant, then “inclusive” is just branding. The literature on database coverage bias, OpenAlex language metadata, and Indigenous data governance gives you plenty of support for making those mechanisms explicit. Use it. citeturn17academia4turn6academia1turn12view1turn13view1

As it stands, this is a **good proposal with real weaknesses**, not a perfect one. I would rate it as conceptually strong, technically promising, and strategically aligned with the challenge, but still vulnerable on operational definition, validation specificity, multilingual retrieval, document-type control, and a small amount of stale technical detail. That is enough to keep it from being the obvious winner. fileciteturn0file0 citeturn18academia3turn6academia1turn18academia0
